# Contamination Flow Field

## Algorithmic Philosophy: "Aquifer Circulation"

Water moves through subsurface geology with an invisible persistence — tracing pressure gradients through porous rock, pooling in basins, diverging around barriers. The contamination that enters this system does not announce itself. It infiltrates. It follows the same ancient paths that groundwater has carved for millennia, but it transforms the character of every molecule it touches. The Aquifer Circulation philosophy asks: what does it look like when a system designed for purity becomes a vehicle for contamination?

The algorithm is a meticulously crafted flow field — a Perlin noise vector field that drives thousands of particles along smooth, water-like trajectories. Each particle is a surrogate for a water molecule tracing its path through an aquifer. The noise function serves as subsurface geology: its valleys and ridges create convergence zones where particles pool, and divergence zones where they scatter. The temporal evolution of the noise field (the slowly drifting z-offset) represents the seasonal shifts that alter groundwater flow — recharge events, drought drawdowns, the slow tectonic creep of deep aquifer systems. The parameters are tuned to produce flow patterns that feel geologically authentic.

Contamination enters the system as radial hotspots — point sources where industrial discharge or landfill leachate has breached the water table. The visual language is thermal: clean water flows in deep oceanic blues (#0a2463, #1e6091, #168aad), while contaminated zones burn through purple into alarm-red (#d62828) and warning-orange (#f77f00). The transition uses a power-law radial falloff (exponent 1.5), mimicking the nonlinear dilution curves observed in real PFAS plumes. Particles that pass through contamination zones carry the stain with them, their trails shifting color as they cross invisible boundaries — a direct analog to how PFAS molecules adsorb to particles and travel downstream.

Overlaid on the accumulated particle traces is a network of monitoring stations — sparse white dots connected by faint lines suggesting a sensor mesh. These are drawn last, on top of the flow field, representing human intervention: the act of measurement imposed upon a system that would otherwise flow unseen. The contrast between the organic, turbulent flow and the geometric monitoring grid is the central tension of the piece — nature's complexity versus our attempt to quantify it. The algorithm runs for exactly 500 frames, accumulating trails through alpha-composite layering until equilibrium, with density, opacity, and velocity balanced so that high-traffic flow corridors glow with accumulated intensity while sparse regions remain whisper-thin.

The conceptual seed is PFAS contamination in U.S. drinking water systems — the subject of the AquaContam research project. The contamination hotspots and the monitoring-station network are stylistic references to that subject matter: the banner is generative art, not a rendering of the data.
